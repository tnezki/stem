STEM II – ES1-II: Define Engineering Problems and Constraints

Essential Standard

Students will analyze engineering problems, identify criteria and constraints, conduct research, and establish measures of success in order to understand what a successful engineering solution must accomplish before designing, building, testing, and improving a prototype.
Learning Target 1: Understanding Engineering Problems

Learning Target: Students will interpret engineering challenges by identifying the problem, purpose, users, goals, and real-world context that shape design decisions.

Challenge Connections: Bridge, Glider, Earthquake Tower, MagLev Vehicle, Landing Rover, Water Rocket Distance, Design Your Own Challenge

  • I can identify the engineering problem that must be solved in a challenge.
  • I can explain the purpose of a design challenge in my own words.
  • I can identify users, stakeholders, or real-world situations connected to the problem.
  • I can explain why the problem matters and what a successful solution should accomplish.
  • I can use the challenge description to determine what information is most important before designing.
Learning Target 2: Researching Scientific and Technical Principles

Learning Target: Students will research scientific concepts, technical ideas, materials, and prior solutions that influence design success.

Challenge Connections: Bridge, Glider, Earthquake Tower, MagLev Vehicle, Landing Rover, Water Rocket Distance

  • I can identify scientific or technical principles that connect to a design challenge.
  • I can gather useful information about materials, forces, motion, structures, stability, energy, or efficiency.
  • I can explain how research affects possible design choices.
  • I can connect scientific principles to expected performance outcomes.
  • I can use research to support early design decisions instead of relying only on guessing.
Learning Target 3: Establishing Criteria and Constraints

Learning Target: Students will identify and use criteria, constraints, scoring systems, budgets, materials, safety rules, and testing requirements to define what counts as a successful solution.

Challenge Connections: Bridge, Earthquake Tower, MagLev Vehicle, Landing Rover, Water Rocket Distance, Design Your Own Challenge

  • I can distinguish criteria, constraints, and trade-offs in an engineering challenge.
  • I can identify required features, limits, rules, budgets, materials, and testing conditions.
  • I can describe how scoring systems or success measures affect design priorities.
  • I can explain how constraints limit possible solutions while still allowing creativity.
  • I can develop measurable success criteria for a self-designed challenge.

Progress Tracking

Not Yet: I need support identifying the problem, criteria, constraints, or science behind a challenge.
Getting There: I can identify most requirements but need more practice connecting them to research and design decisions.
Got It!: I can define the problem, explain the constraints, research relevant principles, and describe what success should look like.

Assessment Methods by DOK Level

DOK 1 — Rungs 1–2

Format: 3–5 minute warm-up, quick check, vocabulary sort, challenge annotation, image prompt, mini-whiteboard response, or exit ticket

Purpose: Check whether students can recognize the basic problem of a challenge and identify the difference between criteria, constraints, and general background information.

Assessment Ideas

  1. Problem Statement Recognition Check Can Be Assessed During Any Major Challenge – Students identify the core engineering problem from a short challenge description.
  2. Criteria or Constraint Sort Can Be Assessed During Bridge or Landing Rover – Students sort statements into criteria, constraints, materials, safety rules, or scoring information.
  3. Success Measure Match Can Be Assessed During MagLev or Water Rocket Distance – Students match challenge goals to measurable outcomes such as distance, time, load, stability, speed, cost, or consistency.
  4. Science Connection Quick Check Can Be Assessed During Glider or Earthquake Tower – Students identify which scientific ideas are most connected to the challenge.
What this tells the teacher:
Students who struggle here may begin building without understanding what the design must accomplish. They may need support reading challenge documents, separating wants from limits, and recognizing that engineering success depends on measurable evidence.
DOK 2 — Rungs 3–4

Format: Exit ticket, annotated challenge sheet, research organizer, requirement checklist, partner check, or short written explanation

Purpose: Check whether students can use challenge requirements and research to explain what a design must do and what factors should influence early design choices.

Assessment Ideas

  1. Bridge Requirements Breakdown Can Be Assessed During Bridge – Students identify the span, material limits, cost factors, testing method, and success measure that define the bridge problem.
  2. Glider Science-to-Design Organizer Can Be Assessed During Glider – Students connect lift, drag, stability, balance, and mass distribution to possible design features.
  3. Landing Rover Constraint Analysis Can Be Assessed During Landing Rover – Students explain how size limits, material costs, egg accessibility, and passive design rules affect possible solutions.
  4. Water Rocket Distance Success Criteria Can Be Assessed During Water Rocket Distance – Students connect launcher compatibility, fins, nose cone, budget, and measured distance to design priorities.
What this tells the teacher:
Students at this level can usually list requirements, but the assessment reveals whether they understand how those requirements shape design. Weak responses often copy rules without explaining why they matter or how they affect engineering choices.
DOK 3 — Rungs 5–6

Format: Constructed response, design brief, research explanation, requirement prioritization, CER-style response, or group whiteboard defense

Purpose: Check whether students can explain how scientific principles, criteria, constraints, and scoring systems interact to shape engineering priorities.

Assessment Ideas

  1. MagLev Problem Analysis Can Be Assessed During MagLev Vehicle – Students explain how levitation, propulsion, mass, velocity, and momentum affect what the vehicle must accomplish.
  2. Earthquake Tower Requirement Prioritization Can Be Assessed During Earthquake Tower – Students rank design requirements and justify which constraints are most important for surviving simulated seismic activity.
  3. Landing Rover Design Brief Can Be Assessed During Landing Rover – Students write a brief explaining the problem, constraints, user need, success criteria, and key science ideas before building.
  4. Research-to-Design Claim Can Be Assessed During Glider or Water Rocket Distance – Students make a claim about which scientific principle should most influence design and support it with evidence.
What this tells the teacher:
This level shows whether students understand the challenge deeply enough to make purposeful design choices. Strong responses explain priorities, trade-offs, and the relationship between science ideas and the scoring system.
DOK 4 — Rungs 7–8

Format: Performance task, design brief critique, challenge proposal, unfamiliar scenario, engineering problem analysis, or capstone planning document

Purpose: Check whether students can transfer ES1 skills by defining or improving the structure of an engineering challenge, especially when requirements are incomplete, conflicting, or student-created.

Assessment Ideas

  1. Critique a Challenge Description Can Be Assessed After Multiple Major Challenges – Students identify missing criteria, unclear constraints, or weak success measures in a sample engineering challenge.
  2. Unfamiliar Engineering Problem Brief Can Be Assessed Before Design Your Own Challenge – Students analyze a new problem and create a design brief with criteria, constraints, research needs, and success measures.
  3. Design Your Own Challenge Proposal Can Be Assessed During Design Your Own Challenge – Students create the problem statement, criteria, constraints, materials, budget, safety rules, testing method, and scoring system for their own challenge.
  4. Criteria and Constraint Defense Can Be Assessed During Design Your Own Challenge – Students defend why their criteria and constraints are fair, measurable, realistic, and connected to the engineering problem.
What this tells the teacher:
DOK 4 tasks show whether students can think like challenge designers, not just challenge participants. Strong students can define a meaningful problem, set measurable success criteria, anticipate limitations, and justify why the testing system will produce useful evidence.

Summative Assessment Plan

Summative Evidence for ES1-II: Students demonstrate proficiency by producing an engineering artifact that defines a problem, explains relevant research, identifies criteria and constraints, and establishes how success will be measured before or during a design challenge.

Recommended Artifact Options:

  • Design Brief: A concise engineering document explaining the problem, users, background research, criteria, constraints, and success measures.
  • Research Poster: A visual artifact connecting scientific principles, materials, and design requirements to the challenge.
  • Problem Analysis Report: A written artifact that breaks down the challenge, identifies priorities, and explains how constraints shape possible solutions.
  • Client Proposal: A persuasive artifact written for a specific audience that explains the need, design goals, constraints, and proposed direction.
  • Challenge Proposal: A student-created design challenge including problem statement, criteria, constraints, materials, budget, safety rules, testing protocol, and scoring system.
  • Engineering Research Infographic: A one-page visual explanation of the science, constraints, and success measures behind the challenge.

Challenge Examples: Bridge Design Brief, Glider Research Poster, Earthquake Tower Problem Analysis Report, Landing Rover Client Proposal, Water Rocket Distance Engineering Research Infographic, or Design Your Own Challenge Proposal.

Suggested Rubric Categories: problem definition, clarity of purpose, identification of criteria and constraints, quality of research, connection between science and design, measurable success criteria, understanding of challenge priorities, and clarity of communication.

Holistic Rubric

A — Highly Proficient

The student clearly defines the engineering problem, identifies meaningful criteria and constraints, explains relevant scientific or technical principles, and establishes measurable success criteria. The artifact shows strong understanding of how research, requirements, safety, materials, budget, and testing conditions shape design decisions.

B — Proficient

The student defines the engineering problem, identifies most important criteria and constraints, and connects relevant research to design decisions. The artifact is clear and complete enough to guide early design work, though some explanations may lack depth or precision.

C — Developing Proficiency

The student shows partial understanding of the engineering problem and challenge requirements. Some criteria, constraints, or research connections may be missing, vague, or copied without explanation, but there is enough evidence that the student understands the basic purpose and limits of the challenge.

Not Yet — Insufficient Evidence

The student does not yet provide sufficient evidence of ES1-II proficiency. The artifact may lack a clear problem statement, meaningful criteria, accurate constraints, relevant research, or measurable success criteria. The student may need reteaching, guided challenge annotation, research support, or reassessment.
