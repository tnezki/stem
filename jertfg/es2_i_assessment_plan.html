STEM I – ES2-I: Develop and Justify Engineering Solutions

Essential Standard

Students will generate, evaluate, and justify engineering solutions using evidence, trade-offs, design criteria, and constraints in order to select and defend a solution before and during the build process.
Learning Target 1: Generating Multiple Design Ideas

Learning Target: Students will create multiple possible solutions to an engineering problem and communicate early design thinking through sketches, labels, explanations, and collaboration.

Challenge Connections: Water Rocket Hang Time, Trebuchet, Zip Line Racer, Design Your Own Challenge

  • I can generate more than one possible solution to the same engineering problem.
  • I can create sketches, diagrams, or models that communicate my design ideas clearly.
  • I can label important design features and explain what each feature is intended to do.
  • I can identify possible advantages and weaknesses of early design ideas.
  • I can build on useful ideas from research, demonstrations, examples, teammates, and previous tests.
Learning Target 2: Evaluating Design Alternatives

Learning Target: Students will compare possible solutions by using criteria, constraints, evidence, and trade-offs to determine which design is most likely to succeed.

Challenge Connections: Trebuchet, Tower Strength, Zip Line Racer, Maximum Distance Racer

  • I can compare multiple design options using the challenge criteria and constraints.
  • I can identify trade-offs between performance, cost, reliability, safety, simplicity, and build difficulty.
  • I can use a decision matrix or similar tool to evaluate competing design ideas.
  • I can explain why one design may be stronger for one goal but weaker for another.
  • I can select a design direction based on evidence rather than preference alone.
Learning Target 3: Justifying Engineering Decisions

Learning Target: Students will defend design decisions by explaining how evidence, research, constraints, and design priorities support the selected solution.

Challenge Connections: Trebuchet, Hydraulics, Maximum Distance Racer, Design Your Own Challenge

  • I can explain why my team selected a specific design direction.
  • I can defend design decisions using evidence from research, testing, examples, or engineering reasoning.
  • I can justify trade-offs made during planning, such as choosing stability over speed or simplicity over complexity.
  • I can explain how criteria and constraints shaped the final design choice.
  • I can communicate design reasoning clearly enough that another person can understand and critique the decision.

Progress Tracking

Not Yet: I need support generating more than one idea or explaining why one design may work better than another.
Getting There: I can compare designs but need more practice using criteria, constraints, and evidence to justify decisions.
Got It!: I can generate, evaluate, and defend design choices using evidence and engineering trade-offs.

Assessment Methods by DOK Level

DOK 1 — Rungs 1–2

Format: 3–5 minute warm-up, sketch sprint, idea list, quick check, mini-whiteboard response, gallery walk observation, or challenge launch prompt

Purpose: Check whether students understand that engineering problems can have multiple possible solutions and can generate more than one idea before choosing a design.

Assessment Ideas

  1. Three-Idea Sketch Sprint Can Be Assessed During Water Rocket Hang Time or Trebuchet Planning – Students sketch three possible design ideas and label one feature that could help each idea succeed.
  2. Multiple Solutions Check Can Be Assessed During Zip Line Racer Launch – Students identify at least two different ways a device might meet the same challenge goal.
  3. Design Feature Match Can Be Assessed During Hydraulics Planning – Students match design features to intended purposes, such as stability, control, lift, speed, or strength.
  4. Idea Source Reflection Can Be Assessed During Any Major Challenge – Students identify whether an idea came from research, a demonstration, a teammate, a previous test, or a previous challenge.
What this tells the teacher:
Students who struggle here may be rushing to the first idea, copying without understanding, or waiting for a teacher-provided solution. They need support with brainstorming routines, sketching expectations, and seeing design as a set of possible choices.
DOK 2 — Rungs 3–4

Format: Exit ticket, design comparison, decision matrix, partner critique, annotated sketch, short written response, or planning conference

Purpose: Check whether students can compare design alternatives using criteria and constraints rather than personal preference alone.

Assessment Ideas

  1. Trebuchet Design Comparison Can Be Assessed During Trebuchet Planning – Students compare two possible launch-arm or base designs and identify which better addresses distance, stability, cost, and reliability.
  2. Tower Strength Decision Matrix Can Be Assessed During Tower Strength Planning – Students score design options using criteria such as height, strength, material efficiency, and build feasibility.
  3. Zip Line Racer Trade-Off Sort Can Be Assessed During Zip Line Racer Planning – Students sort design choices by likely impact on speed, control, safety, and consistency.
  4. Max Distance Racer Alternative Ranking Can Be Assessed During Maximum Distance Racer Planning – Students rank possible chassis, axle, wheel, or weight-distribution ideas and explain the top choice.
What this tells the teacher:
Students at this level can usually describe designs, but the assessment reveals whether they can compare alternatives using the actual challenge goals. Weak responses often show unweighted opinions, missing constraints, or a failure to recognize trade-offs.
DOK 3 — Rungs 5–6

Format: Constructed response, design review defense, decision matrix explanation, peer critique, trade-off analysis, or CER-style justification

Purpose: Check whether students can justify engineering decisions using evidence and explain why a selected solution is stronger than alternatives.

Assessment Ideas

  1. Decision Matrix Defense Can Be Assessed During Trebuchet or Maximum Distance Racer – Students submit a decision matrix and defend why the selected design best fits the scoring system and constraints.
  2. Trade-Off Explanation Can Be Assessed During Zip Line Racer – Students explain a trade-off their team made, such as speed versus control or propulsion power versus reliability.
  3. Hydraulics Design Justification Can Be Assessed During Hydraulics – Students explain why their selected mechanism should improve precision, lift capability, control, or stability.
  4. Rejected Idea Analysis Can Be Assessed During Any Major Challenge – Students choose one rejected design idea and explain why it was not selected, using criteria and constraints as evidence.
What this tells the teacher:
This level shows whether students are making intentional engineering decisions. Strong evidence includes clear comparisons, specific criteria, and reasoning that connects design features to predicted performance. Weak evidence often sounds like “we liked it best” without support.
DOK 4 — Rungs 7–8

Format: Performance task, design review board, critique-and-redesign task, engineering proposal, capstone planning document, or unfamiliar design scenario

Purpose: Check whether students can transfer ES2 skills to unfamiliar or student-created contexts by developing alternatives, evaluating trade-offs, and defending a design direction.

Assessment Ideas

  1. Design Review Board Defense Can Be Assessed During Maximum Distance Racer or Design Your Own Challenge – Students present multiple options, defend the selected design, and respond to critique from peers or teacher.
  2. Critique and Redesign Task Can Be Assessed After Multiple Major Challenges – Students receive a flawed design plan, identify weaknesses, and propose a better solution with justification.
  3. Capstone Solution Proposal Can Be Assessed During Design Your Own Challenge – Students generate several possible solutions for their self-designed challenge and defend which one should be built.
  4. Unfamiliar Challenge Strategy Defense Can Be Assessed After ES2-I Has Been Practiced – Students receive a new engineering problem and create a comparison-based recommendation without building first.
What this tells the teacher:
DOK 4 tasks show whether students can make and defend design decisions independently. Strong evidence here shows that students are not just completing teacher-directed projects; they are thinking like engineers who can weigh options and defend a plan.

Summative Assessment Plan

Summative Evidence for ES2-I: Students demonstrate proficiency by producing an engineering artifact that shows multiple design ideas, compares alternatives, explains trade-offs, and justifies a selected design using criteria, constraints, research, or evidence.

Recommended Artifact Options:

  • Decision Matrix Report: A written or visual artifact comparing multiple design options using weighted or clearly explained criteria.
  • Engineering Sketch Portfolio: A collection of labeled design sketches showing multiple concepts, key features, strengths, weaknesses, and revisions.
  • Blueprint Package: A technical design artifact showing the selected solution, dimensions, materials, key design features, and rationale.
  • Design Review Board Defense: A short spoken, written, or recorded defense where students explain their options, selection process, and trade-offs.
  • Engineering Proposal: A planning artifact recommending a design direction and defending it using criteria, constraints, research, and predicted performance.
  • Design Justification Video: A brief video walkthrough explaining why the chosen design was selected over alternatives.

Challenge Examples: Trebuchet Decision Matrix Report, Zip Line Racer Engineering Sketch Portfolio, Tower Strength Blueprint Package, Maximum Distance Racer Design Review Defense, Hydraulics Engineering Proposal, or Design Your Own Challenge Solution Proposal.

Suggested Rubric Categories: variety of design ideas, clarity of sketches or models, use of criteria and constraints, quality of comparison, recognition of trade-offs, strength of evidence, justification of final design, and clarity of engineering communication.

Holistic Rubric

A — Highly Proficient

The student generates multiple thoughtful design solutions, evaluates alternatives using clear criteria and constraints, recognizes important trade-offs, and strongly justifies a selected solution using evidence and engineering reasoning. The artifact is organized, specific, and demonstrates flexible thinking that could transfer to unfamiliar or student-created challenges.

B — Proficient

The student generates multiple reasonable design solutions, compares alternatives, identifies relevant trade-offs, and justifies a selected solution using appropriate evidence. The artifact is mostly clear and complete. Some explanations may lack depth or precision, but the design decision is supported by criteria and constraints.

C — Developing Proficiency

The student shows partial understanding of design development and evaluation. The artifact may include more than one idea, but comparison may be basic, evidence may be limited, or the final choice may rely too much on preference. There is enough evidence that the student understands that design decisions should be connected to challenge goals.

Not Yet — Insufficient Evidence

The student does not yet provide sufficient evidence of ES2-I proficiency. The artifact may include only one idea, lack meaningful comparison, ignore criteria or constraints, show weak or missing justification, or fail to explain why the selected solution is appropriate. The student may need reteaching, guided brainstorming, decision-making support, or reassessment.
