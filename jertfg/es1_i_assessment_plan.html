STEM I – ES1-I: Define Engineering Problems and Constraints

Essential Standard

Students will analyze engineering problems, identify criteria and constraints, conduct research, and establish measures of success in order to make informed design decisions before building a solution.
Learning Target 1: Understanding Engineering Problems

Learning Target: Students will interpret an engineering challenge by identifying the problem, purpose, stakeholders, and conditions that define a successful solution.

Challenge Connections: Water Rocket Hang Time, Trebuchet, Hydraulics, Design Your Own Challenge

  • I can restate an engineering challenge in my own words without changing its intent.
  • I can identify the problem a design is supposed to solve.
  • I can explain the purpose of the challenge and why the problem matters.
  • I can identify users, audiences, or stakeholders affected by the design.
  • I can describe what a successful solution must accomplish before I begin designing.
Learning Target 2: Researching Scientific and Technical Principles

Learning Target: Students will connect scientific and technical ideas to design decisions by using research to predict which features may improve performance.

Challenge Connections: Water Rocket Hang Time, Zip Line Racer, Hydraulics, Maximum Distance Racer

  • I can identify scientific principles that affect design performance.
  • I can gather useful information from demonstrations, prior tests, examples, and research sources.
  • I can explain how science or technical knowledge influences possible design choices.
  • I can separate useful research from information that is too vague, unrelated, or unsupported.
  • I can use research evidence to support early design ideas before building.
Learning Target 3: Establishing Criteria, Constraints, and Success Measures

Learning Target: Students will define measurable design criteria, identify constraints, and explain how these requirements shape possible engineering solutions.

Challenge Connections: Tower Strength, Trebuchet, Zip Line Racer, Maximum Distance Racer, Design Your Own Challenge

  • I can distinguish criteria from constraints in an engineering challenge.
  • I can identify material, size, budget, safety, and performance limitations.
  • I can define measurable success criteria that can be tested or observed.
  • I can explain how constraints limit possible solutions.
  • I can prioritize competing requirements when a design cannot maximize everything at once.

Progress Tracking

Not Yet: I need support identifying the problem, criteria, constraints, or success measures.
Getting There: I can identify most parts of the challenge but need more practice explaining how they affect design decisions.
Got It!: I can independently define the problem, use research, and explain how criteria and constraints shape the design.

Assessment Methods by DOK Level

DOK 1 — Rungs 1–2

Format: 3–5 minute warm-up, quick check, vocabulary sort, challenge launch exit ticket, mini-whiteboard response, or class discussion prompt

Purpose: Check whether students can recognize the basic parts of an engineering challenge and use foundational EDP vocabulary accurately.

Assessment Ideas

  1. Challenge Vocabulary Sort Can Be Assessed During Water Rocket Hang Time Launch – Students sort statements into problem, criteria, constraint, material, test, or success measure.
  2. What Are We Solving? Can Be Assessed During Trebuchet Launch – Students identify what the team is trying to design and what the design must accomplish.
  3. Criteria or Constraint? Can Be Assessed During Tower Strength Planning – Students classify short challenge requirements as criteria or constraints.
  4. Success Measure Snapshot Can Be Assessed During Zip Line Racer Planning – Students identify how success will be measured and what data will matter later.
What this tells the teacher:
Students who struggle here need more direct support with engineering vocabulary and with separating what the design must do from what limits the design.
DOK 2 — Rungs 3–4

Format: Exit ticket, research note check, annotated challenge brief, partner check, short written response, or planning conference

Purpose: Check whether students can apply challenge information by identifying requirements, connecting research to design choices, and describing measurable success.

Assessment Ideas

  1. Water Rocket Research Connection Can Be Assessed During Water Rocket Hang Time – Students connect one research finding about stability, drag, parachutes, or launch behavior to a possible design choice.
  2. Trebuchet Requirements Breakdown Can Be Assessed During Trebuchet Planning – Students identify required components, restrictions, and the scoring method before sketching solutions.
  3. Hydraulics Science-to-Design Check Can Be Assessed During Hydraulics Research – Students explain how pressure, force, fluid movement, or mechanical advantage affects the design problem.
  4. Max Distance Racer Success Criteria Can Be Assessed During Max Distance Racer Planning – Students describe measurable criteria such as distance, stability, cost, consistency, and material limits.
What this tells the teacher:
Students at this level can usually identify the parts of a challenge, but the assessment reveals whether they understand how research and requirements should guide design choices before building begins.
DOK 3 — Rungs 5–6

Format: Constructed response, annotated design brief, research justification, peer critique, conference defense, or CER-style explanation

Purpose: Check whether students can explain and justify how scientific principles, constraints, trade-offs, and success measures shape the design problem.

Assessment Ideas

  1. Constraint Impact Analysis Can Be Assessed During Tower Strength or Trebuchet – Students choose two constraints and explain how each one limits or changes possible design solutions.
  2. Research Quality Critique Can Be Assessed During Water Rocket Hang Time – Students compare two research notes and decide which is more useful for design planning, then justify the choice.
  3. Criteria Trade-Off Explanation Can Be Assessed During Zip Line Racer or Max Distance Racer – Students explain why maximizing one goal may make another goal harder, such as speed versus stability or distance versus cost.
  4. Hydraulics Problem Analysis Can Be Assessed During Hydraulics – Students explain how the required lift height, load, footprint, and control requirements define the real engineering problem.
What this tells the teacher:
This level shows whether students are moving beyond listing requirements and can explain why those requirements matter. It also reveals whether students are using research as evidence rather than decoration.
DOK 4 — Rungs 7–8

Format: Performance task, challenge proposal, design brief, research-based recommendation, peer review board, or capstone planning document

Purpose: Check whether students can transfer ES1 skills to unfamiliar engineering contexts by defining a problem, developing criteria and constraints, identifying research needs, and defending success measures.

Assessment Ideas

  1. Create-a-Challenge Proposal Can Be Assessed During Design Your Own Challenge – Students write a complete challenge proposal with problem statement, criteria, constraints, materials, safety concerns, testing method, and scoring system.
  2. Unfamiliar Design Brief Can Be Assessed After Multiple Major Challenges – Students receive a new design scenario and develop the problem statement, research questions, constraints, and success criteria.
  3. Client Needs Analysis Can Be Assessed During Design Your Own Challenge – Students identify a user need, translate it into measurable engineering requirements, and justify why those requirements matter.
  4. Research-to-Requirements Defense Can Be Assessed During Capstone Planning – Students defend how their research shaped their criteria, constraints, and testing plan before receiving approval to build.
What this tells the teacher:
DOK 4 tasks show whether students can define engineering problems independently rather than only respond to teacher-written challenges. Strong evidence here shows readiness for open-ended design work.

Summative Assessment Plan

Summative Evidence for ES1-I: Students demonstrate proficiency by producing an engineering artifact that defines the problem, explains relevant research, identifies criteria and constraints, and establishes measurable success criteria before or during a major design challenge.

Recommended Artifact Options:

  • Design Brief: A concise engineering proposal that defines the problem, audience, criteria, constraints, research connections, and success measures.
  • Research Infographic: A visual artifact explaining the science behind a challenge and how research should influence the design.
  • Problem Analysis Report: A written artifact analyzing the challenge requirements, trade-offs, limitations, and measurable goals.
  • Client Proposal: A persuasive artifact written as if proposing a design direction to a client or review board.
  • Design Your Own Challenge Proposal: A capstone artifact where students create the challenge itself, including problem statement, materials, criteria, constraints, safety rules, testing protocol, and scoring system.

Challenge Examples: Water Rocket Hang Time Research Infographic, Trebuchet Design Brief, Hydraulics Problem Analysis Report, Maximum Distance Racer Criteria-and-Constraints Proposal, or Design Your Own Challenge Proposal.

Suggested Rubric Categories: problem definition, criteria and constraints, research quality, connection between research and design choices, measurable success criteria, clarity of communication, and completeness of engineering reasoning.

Holistic Rubric

A — Highly Proficient

The student clearly defines the engineering problem, identifies meaningful criteria and constraints, uses relevant scientific or technical research, and explains how success should be measured. The artifact shows strong reasoning, clear organization, and thoughtful connections between research, requirements, and design decisions. The student can transfer the skill to unfamiliar or student-created challenges.

B — Proficient

The student defines the engineering problem, identifies appropriate criteria and constraints, includes relevant research, and describes measurable success criteria. The artifact is mostly clear and complete. Reasoning is accurate, though some explanations may lack depth, precision, or fully developed connections between research and design choices.

C — Developing Proficiency

The student shows partial understanding of the engineering problem and can identify some criteria, constraints, or research connections. The artifact may be incomplete, too general, or weakly connected to the actual challenge. Some success measures may be vague or difficult to test, but there is enough evidence that the student understands the basic purpose of problem definition.

Not Yet — Insufficient Evidence

The student does not yet provide sufficient evidence of ES1-I proficiency. The artifact may lack a clear problem statement, confuse criteria and constraints, omit meaningful research, fail to describe measurable success, or show little connection between the challenge requirements and design decisions. The student may need reteaching, guided planning, or reassessment.
